Case study · Health & service design

Supporting parents in childcare

What parents need when a child is sick and it is not an emergency.

Role
UX researcher (qualitative) in a graduate team project
Context
2025 · Drexel University · academic concept project
Methods
Semi-structured interviews, empathy and journey mapping, thematic synthesis
Participants
Parents and caregivers
Output
Trust-centered service concepts: nurse hotline, mobile pediatric clinic, companion app
Video
Walkthrough on Vimeo

Context

When a child has a fever at 2am, parents search symptoms, call family and worry. This team project asked what would actually help them in those non-emergency moments. We did not ship a product, but the research with parents was real, and it changed how I think about health technology.

Approach

I designed the interview guide and asked parents to walk me through the last time they worried about their child’s health: real moments, not hypotheticals. We then mapped those stories: where parents went first, what made them trust or distrust what they found, and where they gave up.

Finding

Parents did not want more information. They wanted a person who understood their child and could say what to do next.

One parent described searching a rash at 3am and finding dozens of contradictory answers. That is not a navigation problem; it is a trust problem.

Design response

Rather than another symptom checker, the team designed across the care system to connect families with people:

  • A 24/7 hotline to nurses and pediatric professionals, by phone and text
  • Mobile pediatric clinics for underserved urban and rural areas
  • A companion app that prepares parents before a visit

Project video

What stayed with me

Sometimes the best design is not a feature but access to the right person. This project is the starting point for my PhD question: how parents’ own knowledge of their child could shape the AI systems they turn to for care.
